This is a working set of architecture documents for a bilingual intranet that runs on-premises, on an isolated network. It is published as a worked example. Product names, system names and decision numbers have been replaced with plain words, so what is left is the shape of the thing, not anyone's system.

Three files, all editable:

  • Architecture review deck (PowerPoint, 30 slides) For a board: the value proposition, the target state, four patterns, observability, service levels, the operating model and the risks. Diagrams are native shapes, not pictures.
  • Solution architecture (Word, about 80 pages) The long form: scope, requirements, principles, the business, data, application, technology, digital, information and security views, the patterns, observability and the decision log.
  • Every figure, editable (PowerPoint, 20 slides) One figure per slide, drawn as PowerPoint shapes, so a box can be moved or a label retyped and the figure pasted back into the document.

What is in it

The set answers one question for a review board: can staff find what they need, and can the organization show that the content is current, owned and measured? Everything else follows from that.

  • Find it. One search across the site, in both languages, with quick links staff can set themselves.
  • Trust it. Every page has an owner, an approval workflow and an expiry date. Content is refreshed or retired, not left to go stale.
  • Know what works. Built-in analytics, measured for each language on its own.
  • Grow into it. Two-way features and personalized news come in later phases.

The main parts

Four patterns

A principle says why. A decision says what was chosen. A pattern says how, so the same problem is solved the same way each time. The set carries four.

  • Measure each language on its own. Every number is shown for English and French side by side, so a gap cannot hide inside a total.
  • Check accessibility at every gate. Automated checks where themes are built, where content is published, and on live pages, in both languages. A tool never removes content. A person decides.
  • Publish a status page. One page, served apart from the site, tells staff whether each feature is working.
  • Deliver one ranked queue. One prioritized list per person instead of one email per flag, with bulk decisions and a roll-up by branch.

Watching it, without reaching in

An isolated network makes operations awkward. Security events and health metrics leave through a one-way path, so the teams that receive them cannot look back in. That leaves the people who run the site with nothing of their own. The set adds a small observability platform inside the zone, beside the two feeds.

Service levels

Service levels are only useful if someone can see them. These are deliberately modest for a first release, and they are measured on the platform above rather than in a new reporting tool.

MeasureService level
Availability99.5 per cent in business hours
Page speed95 per cent of pages under 3 seconds, each language counted on its own
Search index delayUnder 15 minutes in each language
Language gapNo more than 10 per cent between English and French on any measure
Silence noticedWithin 15 minutes, in business hours
Local log copy14 days, then the receiving platform owns what it received
BackupsDaily
Recovery point and timeOne week, and one business day

Availability and recovery have to be read together. At 99.5 per cent in business hours the allowed downtime is about fifty minutes a month, so a single outage that used the full one-business-day recovery time would break it. The target is for normal running; the recovery time is the limit for a major failure.

The part that is hardest to fix later

The technology in this set is ordinary and well understood. What decides whether the platform stays healthy is whether anyone is funded to run it after the project closes: someone to patch it, developers who can change it, stewards for taxonomy and the design system, and content owners with time to act on what the reports tell them. On an isolated network nothing refreshes itself, and nothing inside announces that an update exists. That is the risk worth arguing about at a review board, not the choice of search engine.
