Pattern catalog / PAT-014

When we cannot index another system but it has its own search, the reference card can pass the visitor's words to it. The card says what the system is. A button opens that system's search with the words already entered, in its own terms where we know them.

  • Status: Proposed, pending EA confirmation
  • Version 0.1 · 2026-10-24
  • Principles applied: bilingual parity, transparency, secure by design
  • Main layer: Website experience

Supports: Staff reach the right results in another system with their words already entered, instead of starting again.

  • Search and findability
  • Bilingual parity
  • Integration with other systems

Raised by the EA on 2026-10-24 as an extension of the reference card: use the search terms that lead to a card to search the legacy system, converting them to its terms. The shape, steps and trade-offs are the Solution Architect's proposal. Assumed for the journey: the person is a staff member, the scope runs from a search to the old system's results, the other system has a search that takes words in its address, and there is no research yet.

The rule

DO

On a reference card for a system that has its own search, store an address with a slot for the words. When a visitor's search matches the card, offer a button that opens that system's search with their words filled in. Where we hold a crosswalk, change our terms to the system's terms first, and show what was sent.

DON'T

Send visitors to the other system's home page to start again, or change their words without telling them. A silent change hides mistakes, and the visitor cannot tell why the results look wrong.

When this comes up

  • Staff search for a topic held in a system we cannot index yet, such as an old document library with its own search.
  • The card tells them where it is. Then they must retype the words in a second search box.
  • The two systems use different words for the same thing, so the second search finds nothing.

What pulls in different directions:

  • A short path to the answer, against not seeing or controlling what the other system returns.
  • Our words, against theirs.
  • Passing the visitor's words along, against what the other system's logs will record.

Picture it

The experience

A card tells staff where the content is. The handoff takes them there with their words in hand. The old system keeps the content, and keeps the rules about who may see it.

How it works
  1. Check the other system takes a search in its address. Ask its owner for a results page that accepts the words in its web address. Test it with a few words in both languages. If it cannot, use the card alone (Reference card (PAT-003)).
  2. Add the handoff to the card. Three fields: the base address, the name of the words parameter, and any fixed filter, such as one library. The CMS checks the base address against the approved address list.
  3. Build the crosswalk. For each term, list ours (English and French) and the other system's term or terms. Start with the terms that bring most visitors to the card.
  4. Show the button. When a search matches the card, the result and the card both show: Search the old system for the words typed. The label names the destination.
  5. Build the address on the click. Encode the visitor's words, apply the crosswalk, and add them to the base address. Limit the length. Never add the visitor's name or any other detail.
  6. Say what was sent. Show, for example: Searching for permit renewals as licence extension. If there is no mapping, send the visitor's own words. A missing mapping never blocks the handoff.
  7. Count it. The analytics tool counts click-outs per card and per language, and card matches that were not clicked.
  8. Retire it when the content is indexed. When the collection is indexed (Index, don't copy (PAT-001)), search shows the files directly. The card stays as a pointer, and the handoff is removed or kept as Search the original.
Definition cards

Hover or focus any dotted term in this page to see the same definition.

Reference card

A short CMS page that stands for something staff need but we cannot crawl or link to. It says what it is, who it is for and how to reach it, and it has an owner and a review date.

Why it matters hereIt turns an invisible failure (a dead link) into owned, searchable, measurable content.

Crosswalk

A table that matches a term in our words to the term for the same thing in another system's words. It holds each term in English and French.

Why it matters hereVisitors use our words. The other system may use different ones. The crosswalk bridges the two.

Search index

A copy of content organised for fast searching. It holds searchable text and metadata, and a link back to where the content lives.

Why it matters hereOne index per language, so neither language can hide the other.

Journey map

A picture of one person's path through a task, step by step, with what they do, think and feel at each step.

Why it matters hereIt shows where a visitor loses time or heart, so we fix the worst step first.

Service blueprint

A picture of a service in layers: what the person sees, and what staff and systems do out of sight to make it happen. A line of visibility separates the two.

Why it matters hereIt shows the work no visitor sees, and who owns each piece of it.

Approved address list

A short list of web addresses, kept in the CMS, that a card may hand off to. Any other address is refused.

Why it matters hereIt stops a card from sending visitors, and their words, to a place nobody has reviewed.

Analytics tool

A self-hosted tool that counts visits and searches without tracking individuals. It can count each click out of the site, in each language.

Why it matters hereIt shows which old content people look for most, so we know what to index next.

Trade-offs

What we gain

  • Visitors reach results with their words already entered.
  • No crawl, copy or integration is needed. It only needs a search address.
  • The other system applies the visitor's own access rights, so it also covers content we may not index.
  • Small to build: three card fields and a crosswalk table.
  • It shows which old content people want, so we know what to index next.

What it costs

  • The other system must take a search in its address, and keep that address stable.
  • Results are outside our control: language, ranking and accessibility.
  • The crosswalk needs an owner, in both languages. A wrong mapping misleads.
  • The other system's logs record the visitor's words.
  • We count click-outs, not whether people found what they wanted.
  • One more set of fields to test on each review date.

When not to use it

  • The content can be crawled. Index it (Index, don't copy (PAT-001)) so results appear in one search.
  • The system has no search that takes words in its address. Use the card alone (Reference card (PAT-003)).
  • The words must not leave our site, for example sensitive topics, or the system is outside the isolated network. Describe it on the card instead.
Checks every pattern must pass

Bilingual (official languages law)

The card and the button are published in both languages together. The crosswalk holds each term in English and French, and the handoff sends the term in the language the other system supports. Click-outs are counted per language. If the other system works in one language only, the card says so in plain words, in both languages.

Accessible (WCAG 2.1 AA)

The button is a normal link. Its text names the destination and says it opens another system, for example Search the old library for permit renewals, opens another system. The note of what was sent is plain text, not colour or hover. We cannot fix the other system, so the card says where its accessibility is known to fall short.

On-premises, no outside connection

The CMS builds the address, and the visitor's browser opens the other system directly. Only addresses on the approved list are allowed, and only for systems on the isolated network. No outside call is made. A federated-search product that sends the visitor's words to a hosted service would break this constraint, so we do not use one.

How to test a vendor proposal

Ask the vendor to show, not tell. The pattern is met if the answer to each question is yes.

  • Can a card hold a search address with a slot for the words, with no extra module?
  • Does the card refuse an address that is not on the approved list?
  • Does the address builder encode the words and limit their length?
  • Does the page show the words that were sent, including any converted terms?
  • Does a missing mapping fall back to the visitor's own words, with no error?
  • Are click-outs counted per card and per language?
  • Is every part on the isolated network, with nothing sent to an outside service?

Proposed decision record

A reference card may hand the visitor's words to the other system's own search

A reference card for a system with its own search may carry an approved search address. The visitor's words, converted to the other system's terms where a crosswalk exists, are passed in that address, and the words sent are shown. Only addresses for systems on the isolated network are allowed.

Status: Proposed, pending EA confirmation. Not yet logged.

Open questions for the EA

  • Does the legacy system's search accept words in its address? Check the real system and version. The results page of many document platforms does, but confirm.
  • Who owns the crosswalk, and is a short list of the top terms enough to start?
  • Is it acceptable that the other system's logs hold the visitor's words? Does any topic need to be excluded?
  • Should the button also appear on the results page when no card matches? This pattern does not cover that.
  • The journey scores are the Solution Architect's estimate (Assumed). Test them with a few staff before treating them as findings.
  • PAT-014 is assumed to be the next free number.
