1. How to use this

This is a strawman. It is deliberately wrong in places, so that the business has something concrete to correct.

The requirement captured so far is the ability to send email and manage client lists. That is true, and it is not enough to design against: it does not say who is emailed, on what trigger, with whose consent, or how anyone knows the right people received it. Rather than ask for those answers again, this document guesses them.

  • You are not being asked to invent requirements. You are being asked to read a draft and say where it is wrong.
  • Three kinds of mark-up help most: this use case does not exist, this audience rule is wrong, this is missing.
  • Anything not corrected by the date on the assumption log becomes an assumption the design is built on.

What this is not

  • Not a business requirements document.
  • Not a commitment to build everything listed.
  • Not a verdict on anyone's work.

2. What is borrowed, and what is new

This portal shares a platform with the Information Sharing System work, and nothing else. The distinction matters, because a borrowed decision read as a settled one is how a programme inherits rules nobody agreed to.

StatusWhat it covers
BorrowedPlatform patterns: Drupal as the source of truth, GC Notify as the send channel, Solr for content matching, Matomo for measurement.
BorrowedThe mechanics of bounces, unsubscribes and template design. These are properties of GC Notify, not of a programme.
NewEvery use case, audience rule, tier and policy call in this document. The portal is its own programme.
Not carried overThe ISS decision records, its personas and its content calendar. Those were agreed for that programme only.

Scale: 2,500 to 3,000 registered accounts. Comparable to ISS, which is why the same platform choices hold — but the similarity is in size, not in purpose.

3. The product

Every capability in this document is one part of a single loop. The tier chosen for each decides how much of that part gets built.

The email is a short, plain teaser. The real content stays on the portal, behind sign-in. That is not a style preference: GC Notify carries unclassified and Protected A content only, so anything more sensitive has to stay where the client has authenticated.

4. The three tiers

Pick a tier, not a feature list.

Tier 1 — Foundation

The portal can launch and send lawfully. Account email including MFA codes, one consent switch, manual campaigns.

Tier 2 — Targeted

Drupal sends by itself. Preference centre, segmented campaigns, approval workflow, automatic suppression.

Tier 3 — Personalised

Content finds the client. Digests from Solr, saved searches, correction notices, re-engagement, full reporting.

How to read the datasheets

  • Each capability shows the same three tiers. A capability can sit at a different tier from its neighbours — Tier 2 consent with Tier 1 measurement is a legitimate choice, and that is the point of splitting them.
  • Sizes are relative: XS is configuration, S is a few days, M is a sprint, L is more than one sprint with a decision inside it.
  • Select a tier on each card below and the running total at the foot of the page updates. Your selections stay in this browser only; nothing is sent anywhere.

5. Capability datasheets

C-1

Account and identity email

The only capability the portal cannot launch without

MFA makes this a sign-in dependency, not an email feature

With MFA in scope, a client who does not receive the code cannot sign in. GC Notify is a shared cloud service with published service objectives and is explicitly not for emergencies, so sign-in now depends on it.

Tier 1 therefore has to include a documented account-recovery fallback — a help-desk route that does not need the code — and monitoring of the GC Notify status page. Treat that as part of the L, not as a later addition.

C-2

Consent and preferences

The right to send anything at all

Settles decisions D-2, D-3 and D-4

These three shape the consent data model, so they close before Phase 1 is scoped. Tier 1 is viable only if the answer to D-2 is that the programme assigns topics; if clients choose, Tier 2 is the floor, not an upgrade.

C-3

Audience and segmentation

Who a given message is actually for

A segment label is personal information

Targeting by role and sector is a use of personal information under the Privacy Act, and belongs in the privacy impact assessment whichever tier is chosen. Tier 3 adds behavioural data about named clients, which is a further use and may need the assessment reopened.

C-4

Templates and authoring

Where the words live, and who can change them

A limit no tier removes

GC Notify has no API for creating templates. Every template is authored by hand in its web interface at every tier, and the ID is then registered in Drupal. Build the registry at Tier 2 or that manual step becomes the thing that breaks a send.

C-5

Sending, queueing and scheduling

How a message actually leaves the building

Account email cannot stay at Tier 1

Tier 1 here is for campaigns only. Password resets and MFA codes are triggered by the client and have to go through the API, so C-1 at Tier 1 already requires C-5 at Tier 2. These two capabilities are coupled; the others are not.

C-6

Suppression and list health

Keeping the department able to send at all

Tier 1 carries a service-level risk, not just an admin cost

GC Notify can suspend a service when too large a share of a day's sends fail. At Tier 1 that protection depends on a person remembering, every time, inside seven days. With MFA in scope, a suspension also stops sign-in codes.

C-7

Approval and audit

Who authorised this, and what happened

Sent campaigns are records of business value

The Directive on Service and Digital makes a sent campaign a record. At Tier 1 that record is a mailbox, which is not a records system and cannot answer who approved a send after the approver has left.

C-8

Measurement

What the programme can honestly claim

No tier buys open rates

GC Notify reports delivery status only. Opens and clicks do not exist at any tier, and corporate link scanners open every link before the client does, so click counts on an executive segment are close to meaningless. Open rates would mean a separate marketing platform: procurement, a security assessment, and a privacy impact assessment for a new processor.

C-9

Subscription content

The capability that makes the portal worth returning to

S-3 is a policy question before it is a build

If this portal publishes guidance people act on, telling a client that something they read has been corrected may not be optional and may not be opt-out-able. That changes the consent model for everything else, so settle it before choosing a tier here.

C-10

Feedback and reply

The capability nobody has decided on

This is decision D-5, and it has no safe default

The tiers here are not degrees of the same thing. Tier 1 puts replies in a mailbox outside Drupal, outside the audit trail, and caps what can be said at Protected A. Tier 3 keeps the record and the classification but is a build of its own. Choosing a tier here is choosing between three different projects.

6. Tier roll-up

My read, for the product team to argue with

  • Tier 2 is the honest target. Tier 1 is not a smaller version of the product — it is the product with the consent model and the suppression loop left out, and both of those are what keep the department able to send at all.
  • The one place to spend above Tier 2 is C-9, because that is the capability that gives a client a reason to come back.
  • The one place to stay at Tier 1 is C-8, until the programme has said what success means.

The mandatory question

If a client has opted out of all email, how do they learn the privacy notice changed? Two honest answers: a banner they see when they next sign in, or a small, named class of service email that cannot be refused and is disclosed at registration. This is decision D-4, and it changes the consent data model, so it cannot wait until build.

One correction before this reaches the BRD

The working note names PIPEDA. PIPEDA governs private-sector organisations in commercial activity; a federal institution is governed by the Privacy Act. The two have different consent and notice rules.

9. Integration levels

How far Drupal and GC Notify are wired together. The levels map onto the tiers, but they are worth stating separately because they are what the developers will argue about.

LevelWhat staff doWhat Drupal doesFits which streams
0. Notify onlyBuild a list by hand, upload a spreadsheet, send from GC NotifyNothingNone safely — no consent gate
1. Drupal builds the listPick filters in Drupal, export CSV, upload to GC NotifyOwns profiles, consent, unsubscribe links, bounce flagsE-1, E-2, O-1
2. Drupal sendsPick filters, preview, approve, press send in DrupalCalls the GC Notify API per recipient, logs the notification IDAll of T, plus E
3. Drupal orchestratesAuthor a campaign, approve it, read the resultsQueue, schedule, poll delivery status, suppress bounces, build digestsAll streams including S-2 and S-5

My read

Level 1 is not throwaway scaffolding. Levels 2 and 3 run on the same consent model, profiles and audience query — only the last mile changes, from a CSV export to an API call. Level 0 is the throwaway one, and it has no consent gate, which is why it is not a starting point.

The on-premises constraint

Outbound HTTPS from Drupal to the GC Notify API, through the departmental proxy, is the single dependency that can stop the whole design. Polling, not callbacks: an inbound endpoint into on-premises Drupal needs a DMZ and a security review. Firewall changes take weeks, so raise it now rather than at build time.

The argument to make to the project manager

D-5 has no default on purpose

The other eight can proceed on a reasonable assumption and be corrected cheaply. D-5 selects between three different builds, so guessing it wastes a phase. This is the one to escalate if it is not answered.

How this closes without anyone being blamed

  • Every default goes into the BRD and the architecture as a numbered assumption: we will proceed on the basis of X unless the business confirms otherwise by [date].
  • The project manager owns the log and reviews it at every status meeting. That turns a stalled conversation into a tracked risk.
  • When an assumption is confirmed it becomes a decision record. When it is corrected, the correction is the requirement — which is the outcome everyone wanted.
  • D-2, D-3 and D-4 shape the consent data model, so they must close before Phase 1 is scoped. Mark those three as blocking.

13. Running the session

Ninety minutes, four people, no slides in the room at the start.

Asking "what are the requirements?" asks people to produce an abstraction. Asking "what happens next to Marie?" asks them to retrieve an experience. The second is something anyone can do, and it yields the first as a by-product. It also changes whose requirement it is: when they narrate it, it is theirs; when you state it, it is a demand from IT.

The journey, and what each beat is really asking

BeatWhat it surfacesDecision or use case
RegistersWhat you collect, who approves an accountT-1, privacy notice
Confirms her emailDouble opt-in, and what happens if she never confirmsT-1
Picks two sectorsDoes she choose, or does the programme assign? What is on by default?D-2, D-3
Gets a publication alertImmediate or digest, and how oftenS-1, S-2
Forgets her passwordThe launch blocker nobody costs — and with MFA, the sign-in path tooT-3, T-4
Sends feedbackEmail, secure message, or offline. Budget twenty minutes for this beat alone.D-5
Goes quietInactivity, re-engagement, retention. Reliably produces a long silence.D-8, E-5

How to run it

  • Use a name and a specific situation: "Marie, works at a mid-size utility in Saskatchewan, has used the portal twice." Specificity triggers concrete recall; "the user" triggers abstraction, which is the thing to avoid.
  • Your only two lines are "what does she see?" and "then what happens?" Not "what's the requirement" — that is the question that has been failing.
  • Bring the tables out around minute sixty, framed as here is what you just told me, written up. Not as a challenge.
  • End by filling in the decision table together. Nine rows, multiple choice, defaults already marked. Two non-technical people can finish it in one sitting.

The hesitation is the point

A pause means there is no rule yet. Mark it on the drawing — a red dot, a different sticky colour. By the end there should be a dozen, and that constellation is the argument for why the architecture could not start, made by the artifact rather than by you.

The hardest discipline is not filling the silence. You know the answer and will want to supply it. The moment you do, it is your requirement again, and it will not be captured as something anyone owes you.

Two failure modes

  • If they answer everything confidently, you learn nothing. Probe once or twice with "how do we know that?" to tell real knowledge from guessing-to-please.
  • Parked items need an owner named in the room. Otherwise you leave with a dozen red dots and nobody accountable for any of them.
