Client email: journey map and service blueprint

What a registered client goes through when the portal emails them, and the staff work and systems behind each step. All four email features are in scope: Account, Subscription, Engagement and Service.

Web draft No user research yet: every feeling is Assumed One official language first, the second at the file stage Hover or tap a use case ID, such as T-1, for its name

Scope and what this draft rests on

This builds on the capability tiers page. Use IDs such as T-1 or S-3 to find the matching use case there.

Persona and goal

  • A registered portal client. The portal has a few thousand accounts.
  • Goal: hear about the publications and notices that matter to them, and stay in control of what they receive.
  • Client types (role, government or non-government, sector) are To be resolved. One composite client for now.

Scope

  • Starts: the client registers and gets the first email (T-1).
  • Ends: the client changes their topics, unsubscribes, or the account is closed (T-8).
  • Included: Account (T), Subscription (S), Engagement (E), Service (O).
  • Feedback appears in step 7 as a CMS form that owners view and close.

Operations

  • Staff: campaign author, approver, translator, content editor, taxonomy steward, privacy officer, support.
  • Systems: the CMS, a search index and an analytics tool (all on premises), and a shared notification service (cloud).
  • Limits: The notification service accepts only lower-sensitivity data, so emails point to the portal and never carry the content. Outbound access must pass the organization's proxy.

Evidence

  • None yet. No research, complaints, call data or analytics were provided.
  • Every feeling, quote and scene is Assumed or Illustrative.
  • System and policy facts come from the capability tiers page. Staff roles and system owners marked To confirm are Team view.

I assumed these. Correct any that are wrong.

  • The use cases T-1 to T-8, S-1 to S-5, E-1 to E-6 and O-1 to O-3 all exist. Sizes and opt-out rules are not repeated here.
  • The portal has multi-factor authentication (MFA), so the verification code (T-4) is in scope. Confirmed in review. The MFA method (app, text or email code) is to confirm.
  • New accounts get announcements only, and clients choose their own topics (the defaults for D-2 and D-3).
  • A small class of service email cannot be refused (the default for D-4). This changes step 6.
  • Feedback is a form in the CMS. Owners can view and close submissions, and nothing more is designed yet. The client gets no reply. This narrows decision D-5 to "no reply route for now".

Quick guide to the artefacts

This page holds four drawings. Each answers a different question, so choose the drawing by the question.

Journey map

Asks: How does the client experience this over time?

Built from: client research, then stakeholder input.

Used by: product, content, communications, business analysts.

Common mistake: treating assumed feelings as findings.

Service blueprint

Asks: What work and systems make each step happen?

Built from: the journey map, plus staff and architects.

Used by: architects, delivery teams, operations.

Common mistake: drawing systems nobody has named.

Dependency view

Asks: What breaks if one thing stops?

Built from: the support systems lane of the blueprint.

Used by: architects, security, infrastructure.

Common mistake: leaving out the dependencies that have no fallback.

Process flow

Asks: What happens step by step, including when it goes wrong?

Built from: one lane of the blueprint.

Used by: delivery teams, testers, operations.

Common mistake: drawing only the happy path.

The four steps to build them

  1. Discovery. Confirm the client, the scope, the operations and the evidence.
  2. Journey map. Draft it as a web page.
  3. Service blueprint. Draft it as a web page.
  4. PowerPoint and Word files. Build them only after the web draft is approved.

We stop for approval after each step. A web draft is quick to change. Finished files are not.

Team view

After the first review, two changes (MFA and feedback) took minutes in the web draft. The same changes in finished PowerPoint and Word files would mean editing both, in two languages.

Journey map

Emotional curve All points Assumed

The curve shows how a client might feel at each step, from -2 (very frustrated) to +2 (very positive). The line is dashed because no point is backed by research. The lowest point is the first thing to test.

How long each low lasts: unknown. A long wait at the trough matters more than a short dip. Research to confirm: password reset and lockout data from the portal's support log.

Learn: how to read the emotional curve

Score each step by asking how the client would describe it to a colleague: -2 is very frustrated and +2 is very positive. Use research when you have it. When you do not, mark the point as assumed and draw it dashed.

Look for the lowest point and how long it lasts. That is usually the first thing to fix. A flat curve often means the team scored what the service does, not what the client feels.

Eight phases

Each card shows the channel, what the client does, how they might feel, the pain point and what success looks like. Colour always comes with a word and an icon.

Learn: how to write a good phase card

Write the pain point as something the client experiences, not as a system fault. "The email link leads to a sign-in" is better than "SSO redirect".

Friction slows the client. Risk could harm the client or the program. Blocked stops the client. Keep each success line measurable, so a later test can say yes or no.

Storyboard Illustrative

Five key moments. These are plain sketches, not research. The thoughts are Assumed.

Learn: why storyboards, and why they are marked Illustrative

A storyboard helps people who were not in the room picture one moment. Keep the figures plain and avoid stereotypes, including about age or disability.

A thought bubble can only be a research quote or be labelled Assumed. Otherwise a sketch starts to look like evidence.

Key

Positive (+1 or +2). Works well. Always labelled with the word.
Neutral (0). Does the job. No strong feeling.
Friction (-1). Slows or worries the client.
Blocked (-2). The client cannot continue.
Dashed line: assumed. A solid line would mean evidenced.
Icons mark the channel: form, email, web page, sign-in, bell (notice), chat (feedback), sliders (preferences).

What the blueprint would change

  • The client profile needs role, organization type, sector, region and language as fields.
  • The audience query already filters on them (E-2, S-2).
  • Sends to an executive segment may need an extra approval (D-9). To be resolved
  • The privacy impact assessment (PIA) must cover the segment fields, because a segment label is personal information.

To be resolved

  • Personas: not drawn. We need the real client types first.
  • How to resolve: count registered accounts by role, organization type and sector if registration captures them, and ask the business analysts for the five client types that matter most.
  • Delegated access for assistants, and how organization type is confirmed at registration.
Learn: segment or persona?

A segment is a filter, such as "managers in sector X". A persona is a short story about a typical person, built from research. Draw personas only when there is evidence for them. Until then, segments are the safer choice, and sector is usually just a segment.

Dependencies Team view

An arrow points from the system that needs something to the system that provides it. Solid means a live call. Dashed means a batch or poll.

The analytics tool only needs a campaign tag, never a client identity. The CMS checks the consent log in the audience query before any send. A stream that skips that query skips consent.

Learn: how to read the dependency view

An arrow points from the system that needs something to the system that provides it. A solid arrow waits for an answer. A dashed arrow is a batch or a poll.

A box with many arrows into it is a single point of failure. If there is no fallback, say so in words, as the amber label does here.

Process flow: one campaign send

This covers the Engagement and Service streams, and the Subscription digests that go out in batches. The happy path runs along the top. The exception paths loop back or branch.

Learn: how to draw a process flow

Show the happy path first, then the exceptions in their own row. A "Decision:" box has two exits, and each exit says what happened. A loop back to an earlier step needs a label that gives the reason.

What we are learning about these artefacts

Team view

These observations come from building this draft. They are our own judgement, and we should test them as we use more of these artefacts.

A map without research is a hypothesis

Used as a prompt in a stakeholder session, it is useful. It is easier for business analysts to correct a drawing than to write a requirement from scratch.

The blueprint shows what the map hides

The journey map looks fine. The blueprint shows that every send depends on one outbound access rule with no fallback.

Naming real things exposes gaps

Writing every cell as a real screen, role or system found open items: system owners, the MFA method, MFA recovery, delegated access, the feedback owner's tools.

Unknowns can be shown, not hidden

Client types are unknown, so the table shows where they would matter and marks personas "to be resolved". The structure holds while the unknown is closed.

How we could measure the value

  • Which decisions changed because of a drawing?
  • How many gaps did each artefact find that the use case tables did not?
  • How long did the business analysts take to correct a draft, compared with writing from a blank page?
  • Which artefact did nobody open after it was shared?

What this draft does not cover

  • Real client feelings, quotes and the length of each low. Research to confirm: a short interview round with 5 to 8 registered clients, plus the portal's support log for resets and lockouts.
  • Staff effort and service targets. All availability figures say to confirm.
  • Client types and personas. Marked to be resolved until the mix is known.
  • Feedback owner features beyond view and close, such as assigning, tagging, replying or reporting. Suggest a separate UX and product management exploration with the feedback owners.
  • French wording. The English and French files will be separate. French runs 20 to 30 per cent longer, so shapes will leave room.
Before building finished files, three questions to answer:
  1. Are the phases and the emotional curve right? Which pain points are wrong or missing?
  2. Which deck: a detailed reference deck read at a desk, or a briefing deck shown to a room?
  3. Client types are to be resolved. Can the business analysts name the five that matter most, or can registration data show the mix?
