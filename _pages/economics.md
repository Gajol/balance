I keep returning to one idea from engineering: before you design anything, find out what it is made of and what it runs on. Economics is often discussed as money and markets. These notes look under that layer, at the physical things money pays for: energy, food and materials.

Right now this page has one anchor, and it is a big one.

## The world runs on energy

Vaclav Smil's [How the World Really Works]({{ smil.url | relative_url }}) starts from a simple claim: an economy is, at heart, a system for extracting energy and turning it into products and services. One quote from the book says it well: energy is the only truly universal currency.

The scale is easy to miss. An average person today has about 700 times more useful energy at their disposal than someone at the start of the 1800s. In the United States, one person's yearly energy use is like having 240 adults working day and night. Most of that comes from burning fuel, and some uses are very hard to replace. For example, the nitrogen fertilizer that grows much of our food depends on ammonia, and ammonia depends on natural gas as a source of hydrogen.

## Four materials that hold up the modern world

Smil also points to four materials that civilization cannot do without: **ammonia, plastics, steel and cement**. Making each of them takes enormous amounts of energy, and none has an easy low-carbon substitute at scale. That is the heart of his doubt about fast plans for a net-zero world. If seven billion people were to enjoy a European standard of living, the sums on energy and materials do not add up easily.

## Plans, politics and risk

His second theme is people. Humans and governments are poor at handling risks that are slow, large and uncertain, and climate is the clearest example. Democracies also struggle to keep a long-term course across many election cycles. Smil calls some official plans wishful thinking. For instance, he doubts the European target of cutting energy use by half by 2050.

His advice is practical and unglamorous. Do now what is possible, such as raising the efficiency of homes and buildings, rather than wait for a perfect answer.

## What I take from it

I read this book as a counterweight to easy optimism, not as a case for despair. It makes me ask three questions of any big plan: where does the energy come from, what materials does it need, and what happens when politics and risk get involved? Those are good questions for a power grid, and they are good questions for a technology project too.
