# Summary

Vaclav Smil discusses in this book how four fundamental materials (Ammonia, Plastics, Steel and Cement) will limit the ability of us to move a world where 7,000,000,000 people live with the same standard of living at the current energy-consumption levels as Europe and North America.   Understanding globalization and the ironic tragedies it entails like Canada being abundant in trees yet importing toothpicks from China.  

Smil discusses humans and governments inability to deal with risks, especially those with characteristics of climate warming; low-probability and high-impact risks (extreme weather, earthquakes, ..), and low-frequency / high-impact risks (pandemic).  Smil also highlights disproportionate responses to gun violence compared to terrorists attacks.

Smil negates the EU's climate plan as wishful thinking considering the projection is EU citizens will consume 50% less energy in 25 years (2050).  And highlights the inability of democratic governments to make sustained long-term progress.

Smil advises we should do immediately what is possible:

- Raise efficiencies of homes and buildings
- Displace coal-fired electricity with natural gas, taking care to limit methane leakage
- Expand solar and wind generation
- Move away from SUVs and accelerate mass-scale deployment of electric cars

# 1. Understanding Energy

In 2020, the average USA citizen consumes the same energy equivalent to having 240 adults working non-stop, day and night.

- Energy:  The capacity for doing work.

- Forms of Energy: Kinetic, Heat, Elastic, Electrical, Chemical, Radiant, Nuclear, Mass

Energy Substitutions:

- some easy:  candle to light (chemical energy of wax transformed into radiant energy), electric lights powered by electricity generated by steam engines (the chemical energy of fuels transformed into heat, and then into electric energy, and then into radiant energy)

- some difficult: product of nitrogenous fertilizers, requires synthesis of ammonia depends on natural gas as the source for hydrogen.  Hydrogen can come from electrolysis of water, but this is 5X more expensive than deriving hydrogen from methane (natural gas).  A long-distance transatlantic commercial aircraft using electricity powered-flight is unrealistic.

- energy (scalar), not to be conflated with power (energy per unit of time).
- energy densities : critical for energy storage and portability

### Crude Oil

Crude oil:  Refinement produces specific fuels (gasoline - lightest, and residual fuel oil - heaviest), and also lubricants, and asphalt (paving and roofing).

- production of crude oil started in 1850s in Russia, Canada and the US.    Discovery of giant oil fields in Middle East and USSR in 1920's.  Most discovered post WWII (Saudi Arabia primarily).  Largest Russian ones discovered in 1948 and 1965.
- crude oil was so cheap, there were no incentives to use it efficiently (single glazed window, no insulation, ...)
- 1950 US produced 50% of world's crude oil.
- 1960 OPEC founded
- 1971 : Algeria, Libya, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar and Saudi Arabia nationalized their oil fields.
- 1973 OPEC raised price of oil to $3.01/barrel, followed by 17% raise by six Arab Gulf states.
- 1973 Israeli victory over Egypt in Sinai; OPEC embargoes all oil exports to US.  [^1]
- Two waves of oil price rises:
  - 1974 Oil at $11.65/barrel - 450% rise in one year:  End era of rapid economic expansion
  - 1978 Fall of Iranian monarchy, takeover of Iran by a fundamentalist theocracy.  Oil went from $13 to $34/barrel from 1978 to 1981.
- 1986 $13/barrel: Globalization centered around China.  Deng Xiaoping economic reforms and massive foreign investment.  

### Electricity

Electricity is difficult to store, had high costs to produce and has many technical challenges (transformers, transmission lines, final use devices).  There are many benefits of electricity:

- the use of electricity at final consumption is effortless and clean
- exceptionally efficient
- no bulky storage
- no carrying and stoking
- no dangers of incomplete combustion releasing poisonous gas (CO)
- no clean-up of lamps, stoves, furnace

The service sector dominates the modern economy, and is reliant on electricity (elevators, trash compactors, mobile phone/vibrator motor (4mm x 3mm), electric pumps for water supply).

Electrification:  Converting fuels to electricity.

### Decarbonization

Based on known discoveries coal can last another 120 years and oil gas another 50 years.  

The carbon neutrality discussion relies on a yet to be discovered mass technology to capture and store carbon indefinitely.   Planting trees is a temporary measure.   

Nations are decarbonizing electricity, however electricity is only a fraction of the nation's total energy demand.  Nations like Denmark have about 45% of electricity generated by renewables (wind), but Denmark relies on Sweden (nuclear, wind) and Germany (various) to cover shortfalls on generation.   Larger countries, like Germany, cannot rely on Denmark, as Germany's demand is 20X that of Denmark, and Denmark would be unable to provide sufficient electricity to cover Germany's shortfall.

Transmission of electricity across a nation is difficult, and in USA/Canada there are no suitable long-terms plans.  Difficulties include infrastructure costs, not-in-my-backyard easements.

Nuclear can operate 90% of the time.  USA/Canada had small, safe, modular reactors of the 1980's, but these have not been commercialized.  The access to cheap natural gas has lowered the need for nuclear.

China, India and South Korea are committed to nuclear.   Whereas Germany had committed to be nuclear free by 2022.

Storage of Electricity.  The only viable storage is a Pump Hydro Storage (PHS) system which on off-hours pumps water to a higher elevation to then allow it to fall again in the peak demand.  This uses about the 25% of the generated electricity, and relies on natural elevation for the storage.  The most efficient batteries (Li-Ion) are able to store about 400 Watt-hours/kilogram (Wh/kg).   This is about 30X less than the energy-per-kilogram of jet kerosene (12,000 Wh/kg).

Fossil fuels still provide the majority of the total energy demand:

| Nation  | Fossil Fuel Percent of Total Demand |
| ------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Germany | 78%                                 |
| China   | 85%                                 |
| Japan   | 90% : Fukushima loss                |

Global fossil consumption is 10 billion tons/year (10,000,000,000 tons/year).   This is two-times the mass of water consumed by the global population of 8 billion people.   Any rapid substitution is impossible; this is an engineering and scientific reality.

Long-term energy supply scenarios:   Stated Policies scenario published by International Energy Agency (IEA) predicts the fossil fuels share of global demand declining from 2019 80% to 72% by 2040.  

- The IEA's Sustainable Development Scenario sees fossil fuels supplying 56% of global primary energy demand by 2040.  This is the most aggressive decarbonization scenario. [^2]

## Two Centuries Ago

A shift (epochal) the addition of non-solar forms of energy began to affect crop production, and later the capture of wild marine species.   Case study on USA (could have chosen China, Japan, East Asia, ...)

1801: Wheat grown in NY same way as in England and similar to ancient Egypt 2,000 years ago.

- planting:
  - two oxen, wooden plow with iron plate
  - seed from last year's crop, sown by hand
  - brush harrows used to cover seeds
  - 27 hours labour / hectare, 35 ox hours
- harvest (securing the crop):
  - cut with sickles
  - stalks bundled and tied manually in sheaves
  - stacked upright to make shocks or stooks
  - left to dry
  - hauled to barn, and threshed by flailing them on a hard floor
  - straw is stacked
  - grain is winnowed (separated from the chaff), measured and put into sacks
  - 120 hours labour / hectare, 35 ox hours
  - 83% of US population were farmers in 1800
- yield
  - one tonne of grain, 10% set aside for seed
  - 10 minutes of human labour to produce a kilogram of wheat, which will yield 1.6 kilograms of bread (two loaves)

1901: one hundred years later, most wheat comes from Great Plains.  Example, Red River Valley, North Dakota

- four horses pull gang (multi-share) steel plows and harrows
- mechanical seed drills plant, mechanical harvesters cut the stalks and bind the sheaves
- threshing machines are powered by steam engines
- grain is taken to granaries
- saves about 22 hours per hectare, about 1/7 of the time it did in 1801
- use of draft animals is about 37 horse-hours per hectare (about 2 minutes / kilogram of grain)
  - Impact: about 25% of American farmland is devoted to growing fodder for draft animals.

- inorganic fertilizers are making inroads, importing Chilean nitrates and Florida phosphates
- USA stopped counting draft animals in 1961

2021: Kansas is the leading wheat-growing state; use case Arkansas River Valley.

- farms are 3-4X larger than a century ago
- most work is done by 1 or 2 people and large machinery (400 horsepower+)
- seed comes from certified growers
- plants receive fertilizers; nitrogen (applied as ammonia or urea), and protection from insects, fungi and weeds)
- harvesting by large combines transferring grain directly to trucks to be transported to storage silos and sold around the country or shipped to Asia or Africa.
- 2 hours labour / hectare and 3.5 tonnes / hectare  (two seconds labour / kilogram of grain; from 10 minutes in 1801)

### Agrochemicals

Agrochemicals fuel needs dwarfs the fuels required to power farm machinery.

- fungicides, herbicides, insecticides are high-energy-intensive products.  Applied in small quantities (fractions of kilograms / hectare)
- fertilizers, supplying key macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) require less energy to produce, but are needed in large quantities
  - potassium: least costly, potash (KCl) from surface or underground mines
  - phosphate: mine phosphates and process to to make superphosphate compounds
  - ammonia: compound for making nitrogen fertilizers.  Wheat, rice and many vegetables require 100-200 kilograms / hectare.  These high-needs make nitrogenous fertilizers the most important indirect energy input in modern farming.

### Leguminous Food Crops

Leguminous crops can fix their own nitrogen supply (soybeans, beans, peas, lentils, peanuts).   They have a natural bacteria that can convert non-reactive nitrogen to reactive nitrogen and ammonia (NH<sub>3</sub>).   Cover crops (alfalfa, clovers and vetches) are also capable of creating their own nitrogen supply.  Their roots host bacteria capable of converting inert atmospheric nitrogen to nitrates.

- no staple grains, no oil crops (except soybeans and peanuts) and no tubers can produce their own nitrogen.   Crop rotation with an alfalfa, clover or vetch for a few months and till into soil to replenish reactive nitrogen is required.   Traditional agriculture spread manure (laborious and inefficient, low nitrogen)
- cities capture human waste and spread it on fields.  This consumed 20-30% of labour in cropping.

### Green Revolution

1909, Fritz Haber invented ammonia synthesis.  The Green Revolution relied on higher nitrogen applications.

## Ammonia

Ammonia (NH<sub>3</sub>), is in the top position, as it would be impossible to feed 40% of today's population without it.  Ammonia is an existential constraint that does not apply to cement, steel or plastic.

Synthesis of ammonia is difficult.

- Accidental Discoveries:  inventions have led to accidental discoveries, Teflon.  Tetrafluoroethylene was used as a refrigerant compound.  When stored in refrigerant cylinders, it polymerized and turned into polytetrafluorethylene (white, waxy, slippery powder).  Post WWII Teflon was the best-known synthetic material.
- Deliberate Discovery:  Between 1850 to1900 the population of industrialized countries of Europe and North America grew from 300 million to 500 million.  The need for more food, and crop yields remained stagnant.  In this time period 200 million hectares of North & South America, Russia and Australia were converted to grain fields.   
- Phosphates mined first in North Carolina and then in Florida.
- Ammonia started with mining of guano from dry tropical islands, and importing Chilean nitrates.

Synthesis invented in 1908 by Fritz Haber a professor of physical chemistry and electrochemistry, supported by BASF.  Solution used an iron catalyst and deploying a reaction pressure.

- scaling this process was a major feat.   Carl Bosch (an chemical and metallurgical engineer) from BASF succeed in four years.   First ammonia synthesis plant in Oppau 1913.  The process is known as the **Haber-Bosch process**[^3].  Spreading manure was still most popular method even in the 1950's.  Haber-Bosch synthesis of ammonia is perhaps the most momentous technical advance in history
- Green Revolution started in the 1960's enabled by ammonia production (30 million tons).  Key innovations was using natural gas as source of hydrogen, and centrifugal compressors, and better catalysts.

### China

Post-Mao China took the lead in ammonia production:

- Mao was responsible for the deadliest famine in history 1958-1961.  Mao, declared communist state in 1949, died in 1976.
- China ordered 13 ammonia urea plants from M.W. Kellogg (Texas) after President Nixon's trip to China Beijing in 1972.
  - 1972 China abolished food rationing
  - 2000 China's data per capita food supply was higher than Japan's.
  - 60% of China's ammonia is synthetically produced

Conserving Ammonia

Spreading ammonia on fields results in losses of ammonia (volatilization).  The only effective solutions to field losses are:

- spreading expensive slow-release compounds
- using precision farming; applying fertilizers based on analyses of soil

### Africa

Africa is the fastest growing population and does not have access to ammonia.  Africa is a food importer.  For Africa to be food self sufficient will require the use of ammonia.   

### Hopeful Invention

- Radical: Genetic engineering create a non-leguminous plant with nitrogen-fixing capabilities.
- Less Radical:  Inoculate seeds with bacterium for the generation of nitrogen

## Plastics

Plastics are fit for moulding/forming.  Two key monomers are ethylene and propylene are produced by steam cracking (heating to 750-950<sup>o</sup>C).

- thermoplastics: soften when heated, dominate global output.  
  - 20% low and high-density polyethylene (PE)
  - 15% polypropylene (PP)
  - 10% polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
  - applications: heavy-duty pipes, flanges, anti-skid surfaces, chemical tanks, car interiors and exteriors, flame-retardant applications
- thermoset plastics: resist softening when heated.   Polyurethanes, polyimides, melamine and urea-formaldehyde

PET - Polyethylene terephthalate : drink bottles (patented in 1941)

Applications:

- everywhere, first application was gear-shift knob on a Rolls-Royce.
- indispensable roles in health care. PPE (personal protection equipment); gloves, masks, shields, gowns, booties.  Flexible tubes, catheters, intravenous and blood bags, sterile packaging, bedpans, rails, thermal blankets, labware.

Environment: *This irresponsible dumping of plastics is not an argument against the proper use of these diverse and often truly indispensable synthetic materials.*

## Steel

Steel:

- alloy dominated by iron (Fe)
- 3,500 varieties of steel
- Pig or cast iron is 96% iron, 1-4% carbon and 0.5-3% silicon (Si) produced in blast furnaces:
  - high carbon makes it brittle
  - low ductility (ability to stretch)
  - low tensile strength compared to bronze or brass

Four categories of steel:

- Carbon steels: 90% of all steels are 0.3 to 0.95% carbon): bridges, fridges, gears and shears

- Alloy steels: one or more elements of manganese, nickel, silicon, chromium, aluminium, molybdenum, titanium and vanadium:  Added to improve a physical property: hardness, strength, ductility.

- Stainless steel: 10-20% chromium.  Surgical instruments, engines, machine parts and construction

- Tool steels: 2-4X tensile strength of construction steels.

Steel is used everywhere; oil rigs, cars, buildings.  Can we secure an adequate supply of steel?

- do we have adequate supplies of iron ore?  Yes, iron is Earth's dominant element abundant in the crust and core.  Only oxygen, silicon and aluminium are more common.
- can we supply enough to raise the standard of living in low-income countries?
- is steelmaking environmentally friendly?
- can we produce steel without using fossil fuels?

Recycling steel is possible with an electric arc furnace (EAF).  1,800<sup>o</sup>C electric arcs.  An EAF consumes as much electricity as a USA city of 150,000 people.

The energy requirement of producing steel (2019) is about 34 exajoules : about 6% of worlds primary energy supply.  Producing steel includes new steel and recycling steel.

## Cement

Concrete: a world created by cement.

Cement: created by heating limestone to 1,450<sup>o</sup>C with clay, shale or waste materials in kilns (100-200m long).  This clinker (fused limestone and aluminosilicates) is ground to yield fine, powdery cement.

Concrete: 65-85% aggregates and water 15-20%.

- finer aggregates (sand) result in stronger concrete
- mixture is held together by cement : ~15% of concrete's final mass.  Cement reacts to water and hardens the concrete.

1824 Joseph Aspdin patented modern cement

- firing limestone & clay; the clinker was glasslike and looked like limestone from the Isle of Portland
- Portland cement.

1884 Ernest Ransome invented reinforcing steel bars (re-bar)

Robert Maillart: Swiss engineer invented concrete bridges.  Zuoz 1901 and Tavanasa 1906.

Concrete

- bridges

## Words

Hover over a highlighted word in the notes above to see its meaning.

- [**arbitrage**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/arbitrage): buying and selling the same thing at nearly the same time in different markets to profit from a price difference. *Labour arbitrage* is the same idea applied to jobs: moving work to wherever labour costs least.
- **arid**: too dry to support farming; very little rainfall.
- [**anthropogenic**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/anthropogenic): caused by human activity, such as anthropogenic CO<sub>2</sub> emissions.
- **category mistake**: treating something as if it belonged to a different category. Example: comparing how fast phones replaced landlines (an "electronically enabled world") with how fast we could replace terawatts of power generation, water, crops and raw materials supplied to billions of people.
- [**concomitant**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/concomitant): something that goes along with, or comes as a side effect of, something else. *"Certainly, the affluent world, given its wealth, technical capabilities, high level of per capita consumption and the concomitant level of waste, can take some impressive and relatively rapid decarbonization steps."*
- **energy**: the capacity to do work. A scalar, measured in joules.
- **globalization**: *the growing interdependence of the world's economies, cultures, and populations, brought about by cross-border trade in goods and services, technology, and flows of investment, people, and information.* [Peterson Institute for International Economics](https://www.piie.com/microsites/globalization/what-is-globalization)
- [**immiseration**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/immiseration): the process of making people poorer or more miserable.
- [**lipid**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/lipid): any of a group of organic compounds, including fats, oils and hormones, that do not mix with water.
- **natural gas**: a fuel made mostly of methane (CH<sub>4</sub>).
- **power**: energy per unit of time (a rate). Watts = joules per second.
- *primus inter pares*: Latin for "first among equals".
- [**proscription**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/proscription): a ban or restriction. *"Given the religious proscriptions on the consumption of pork and beef, chicken is the only universally favored meat."*
- **R/P (reserves-to-production ratio)**: how many years a resource would last at today's rate of production. About 50 years for crude oil and 300 years for iron ore.
- **scalar**: a quantity described only by its size, with no direction. Volume, mass, density, time, speed and energy are scalars.
- **singularity**: a word with several meanings. In maths, a point where a function becomes infinite. In the study of unstable systems, where [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell) first used it in 1873, a situation where arbitrarily small changes may lead to arbitrarily large effects. In futurism, [Ray Kurzweil's](https://singularityhub.com/ray-kurzweil/) prediction that by 2045 machine intelligence will surpass human intelligence and the two will merge. See also [Singularity (system theory)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_(system_theory)).
- [**strident**](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/strident): harsh, loud and insistent.
- **WWW (wind, water and solar)**: the main renewable energy sources.
- **Common Era (CE) and Before Common Era (BCE)**: the same calendar as AD and BC. 400 BCE and 400 BC are the same year. See [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era).

## Technical Terms

More terms from the notes, grouped by theme. They are also underlined in the text above.

### Energy and electricity

- **joule**: The standard unit of energy. Lifting a small apple about one metre takes roughly one joule.
- **MJ (megajoule)**: One million joules.
- **exajoule (EJ)**: One billion billion joules (10<sup>18</sup>). Used for world-scale energy totals.
- **Wh/kg (watt-hours per kilogram)**: How much energy a battery or fuel stores for its weight.
- **energy density**: Energy stored per unit of volume (or, loosely, of weight). It decides how easy a fuel is to store and carry.
- **pumped hydro storage (PHS)**: Using spare electricity to pump water uphill, then letting it fall through turbines at peak demand.
- **small modular reactor**: A small nuclear reactor built in a factory and shipped to site, instead of built from scratch on site.
- **decarbonization**: Cutting the carbon dioxide released by an activity, a sector or a whole economy.
- **electrification**: Switching from burning fuel to using electricity.
- **carbon capture**: Trapping carbon dioxide from smokestacks or the air and storing it, so it stays out of the atmosphere.
- **firm power**: Electricity that is available on demand, whatever the weather or time of day (for example nuclear, hydro or gas).
- **net zero**: Removing as much greenhouse gas as you release, so the net addition is zero.
- **IEA (International Energy Agency)**: The Paris-based agency that publishes the World Energy Outlook and other energy forecasts.
- **OPEC**: The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, a group of oil producers that coordinates oil output and prices. Founded in 1960.
- **Stated Policies Scenario**: An IEA forecast of where energy goes if governments only do what they have already announced.
- **Sustainable Development Scenario**: An IEA forecast of the most aggressive path to cutting emissions.
- **Fukushima**: The 2011 nuclear accident in Japan after an earthquake and tsunami. Japan shut reactors afterwards and burned more fossil fuel.

### Food and farming

- **pelagic**: Living near the surface of open water, like anchovies, sardines and mackerel.
- **aquaculture**: Farming fish and shellfish.
- **leguminous**: Describes plants (beans, peas, lentils, soybeans, peanuts) whose root bacteria make their own nitrogen.
- **nitrogenous fertilizer**: A fertilizer that supplies nitrogen, such as ammonia or urea.
- **Haber-Bosch process**: The industrial way to make ammonia from nitrogen in the air and hydrogen (usually from natural gas), using high pressure and an iron catalyst.
- **volatilization**: Ammonia escaping into the air as a gas instead of staying in the soil.
- **eutrophication**: Too many nutrients (phosphorus, nitrogen) in water, causing huge algae growth that uses up the oxygen.
- **anoxic**: Without oxygen.
- **agrochemicals**: Chemicals used in farming: fertilizers, herbicides (weeds), insecticides (insects) and fungicides (fungi).
- **potash**: Potassium salts, mostly potassium chloride (KCl), mined and used as fertilizer.
- **superphosphate**: Fertilizer made by treating phosphate rock with acid so plants can use the phosphorus.
- **cover crops**: Crops such as alfalfa and clover grown to protect and feed the soil, then ploughed in.
- **draft animals**: Horses, oxen and mules used to pull ploughs and wagons.
- **hectare**: 10,000 square metres, about 2.5 acres.

### Materials

- **electric arc furnace (EAF)**: A furnace that melts scrap steel using electric arcs, used to recycle steel.
- **clinker**: The hard lumps made by heating limestone and clay in a kiln. Ground up, they become cement.
- **Portland cement**: The common modern cement, patented in 1824. Named after limestone from the Isle of Portland.
- **aggregates**: Sand, gravel and crushed stone mixed into concrete.
- **re-bar**: Steel bars set inside concrete to strengthen it.
- **alloy**: A metal mixed with other elements to change its properties. Steel is an alloy of iron.
- **thermoplastic**: Plastic that softens when heated and can be re-moulded.
- **thermoset plastic**: Plastic that sets permanently and does not soften when heated again.
- **monomer**: A small molecule that links with others into long chains (polymers), the building block of plastics.
- **steam cracking**: Heating hydrocarbons with steam to very high temperatures to split them into ethylene and propylene.
- **polymerized**: Joined into long chains of repeating molecules.
- **nacelle**: The housing on top of a wind turbine tower that holds the gearbox and generator.
- **turbofan**: A jet engine with a large front fan. Quieter and more efficient than a plain turbojet.
- **turbojet**: An early type of jet engine where all the air goes through the engine core.
- **chronometer**: A very accurate clock used to find longitude at sea.

### Globalization and economy

- **GDP (gross domestic product)**: The total value of goods and services a country produces in a year.
- **foreign direct investment (FDI)**: Money invested in a business, factory or property in another country.
- **offshoring**: Moving work or production to another country.
- **reshoring**: Bringing work or production back to the home country.
- **de-risking**: Reducing dependence on a single supplier or country.
- **supply chain**: The linked steps and suppliers that take a product from raw material to the customer.
- **bulk carrier**: A ship that carries loose cargo such as ore, grain or coal.
- **containerization**: Shipping goods in standard sealed boxes that move between ship, train and truck.
- **Moore's Law**: The observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years.
- **Modern Monetary Theory (MMT)**: An economic view that a government issuing its own currency cannot run out of money, so debt ratios matter less than inflation and real resources.
- **WTO (World Trade Organization)**: The body that sets and oversees trade rules between countries.

### Risk, climate and environment

- **psychometric scale**: A way of measuring how people rate risks, for example by how dreadful or unknown they feel.
- **biosphere**: The parts of Earth where life exists.
- **greenhouse gas**: A gas, such as carbon dioxide or methane, that traps heat in the atmosphere.
- **ozone layer**: A layer high in the atmosphere that shields us from ultraviolet radiation.
- **CFC**: Chlorofluorocarbon: a chemical once used in fridges and sprays that damaged the ozone layer.
- **Montreal Protocol**: The 1987 treaty that phased out CFCs.
- **Paris Accord**: The 2015 international climate agreement.
- **CVD (cardiovascular disease)**: Disease of the heart and blood vessels.
- **PPE (personal protective equipment)**: Gloves, masks, gowns and similar gear.
- **Carrington Event**: The huge solar storm of 1859 that disrupted telegraph systems worldwide.
- **coronal mass ejection**: A giant burst of plasma and magnetic field thrown out by the Sun.
- **Great Oxidation Event**: The time, about 2.5 billion years ago, when oxygen first built up in Earth's atmosphere.

### People

- **Vaclav Smil (born 1943)**: Czech-Canadian scientist, professor emeritus at the University of Manitoba. Writes about energy, food, materials and risk, backed by numbers. Respected and widely read. Critics say he is too sceptical about fast energy change. *Verdict: mostly positive.*
- **Robert Ayres (born 1932)**: American-born physicist and economist. Argued that energy, not just labour and money, drives economic growth. Respected in ecological economics. Mainstream economists have mostly set his energy view aside. *Verdict: positive, but niche.*
- **Fritz Haber (1868 to 1934)**: German chemist. Invented ammonia synthesis in 1908, which made modern fertilizer possible, and won the 1918 Nobel Prize. Also led Germany's chemical weapons work in World War I. Feeds billions of people, but is also tied to poison gas. *Verdict: mixed.*
- **Carl Bosch (1874 to 1940)**: German engineer at BASF. Turned Haber's lab method into a factory process (first plant at Oppau, 1913) and shared the 1931 Nobel Prize. His process fed the world. His later leadership of the IG Farben chemical group is criticized. *Verdict: mostly positive.*
- **Richard Nixon (1913 to 1994)**: US president from 1969 to 1974. His 1972 visit to China opened relations after decades apart. Resigned in 1974 over Watergate. Praised for opening China, criticized over Watergate and Vietnam. *Verdict: mixed.*
- **Deng Xiaoping (1904 to 1997)**: China's leader from 1978. Started market reforms and opened China to trade and foreign investment, lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty. Credited with China's economic rise. Criticized for ordering the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown. *Verdict: mixed, mostly positive on the economy.*
- **Joseph Fourier (1768 to 1830)**: French mathematician. In the 1820s he showed that Earth's atmosphere keeps the planet warmer than it would otherwise be. An early step toward the greenhouse effect. Honoured as a founder of climate science. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Eunice Foote (1819 to 1888)**: American scientist. In 1856 her experiments showed that carbon dioxide and water vapour trap heat. Largely forgotten for over a century, now recognized as a pioneer. *Verdict: positive, credit came late.*
- **John Tyndall (1820 to 1893)**: Irish-born physicist. Measured how water vapour and carbon dioxide absorb heat, explaining the greenhouse effect. Honoured as a founder of climate science. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Roger Revelle (1909 to 1991)**: American oceanographer. His 1957 paper with Hans Suess warned that humans were running a huge experiment by adding carbon to the atmosphere. Helped start the CO<sub>2</sub> measurements at Mauna Loa. Honoured as an early voice on climate change. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Hans Suess (1909 to 1993)**: Austrian-American chemist. With Revelle, showed that fossil-fuel carbon was building up in the air. Honoured as an early voice on climate change. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Joseph Aspdin (1778 to 1855)**: English bricklayer who patented Portland cement in 1824, the base of modern concrete. Remembered as the inventor of modern cement. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Ernest Ransome (1852 to 1917)**: English-American engineer. Pioneer of reinforced concrete, with steel bars set inside. Remembered as a builder of modern concrete construction. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Robert Maillart (1872 to 1940)**: Swiss engineer who designed light, graceful concrete bridges, such as the Salginatobel Bridge (1930). Celebrated as a master of concrete design. *Verdict: positive.*
- **John Harrison (1693 to 1776)**: English clockmaker. His sea clock (the chronometer) let ships find their longitude, a big step for safe ocean travel. Celebrated as solving the longitude problem. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Chauncey Starr (1912 to 2007)**: American nuclear engineer. His 1969 paper compared voluntary and involuntary risks, and started modern risk analysis. Respected as a founder of risk analysis. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Osama bin Laden (1957 to 2011)**: Founder of al-Qaeda. Behind the 9/11 attacks in 2001, which led to the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. Condemned for mass murder. *Verdict: overwhelmingly negative.*
- **The Wright brothers (Wilbur 1867 to 1912, Orville 1871 to 1948)**: American inventors who made the first powered, controlled airplane flight in 1903. Celebrated as aviation's founders. *Verdict: positive.*
- **Francisco Franco (1892 to 1975)**: Spain's dictator from 1939 until he died. Ruled through repression, and the country stayed poor and isolated for years. Remembered mainly for repression and civil-war killings. *Verdict: mostly negative.*
- **Hannah Ritchie**: Scottish data scientist at Our World in Data. Author of *Not the End of the World* (2024). Widely praised for clear, data-based writing. Some critics think she is too optimistic. *Verdict: mostly positive.*
- **Mao Zedong (1893 to 1976)**: founded the People's Republic of China in 1949 and led the Communist Party until his death. He united the country after decades of civil war and foreign invasion, with gains in land reform, literacy and health. But the Great Leap Forward (1958 to 1962) caused the deadliest famine in history, and the Cultural Revolution (1966 to 1976) brought widespread persecution. History's verdict is mixed: mostly negative abroad, while the Party in China still honours him and officially calls his later years mistakes. See [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong).
