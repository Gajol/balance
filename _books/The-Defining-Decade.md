*The Defining Decade: Why Your Twenties Matter and How to Make the Most of Them*, by Meg Jay.

- Author: [Meg Jay](https://megjay.com/about/), clinical psychologist
- Book: [*Jay, Meg. The Defining Decade: Why Your Twenties Matter and How to Make the Most of Them Now. First revised trade paperback edition. New York: Twelve, 2021*](https://worldcat.org/en/title/1241709696).

# Summary

The book describes the challenges young adults face in their twenties.  The epilogue sums it up best: there is no formula for a good life, and no single right or wrong path.

> __Am I going to make it?__
>
> *I don't know.  You haven't decided yet.*

Meg Jay wrote the book to help people in their twenties.  She describes the changing world, expectations and pressures they face, and her advice is to be deliberate in your choices.  Her TED talk, [Why 30 is not the new 20](https://www.ted.com/talks/meg_jay_why_30_is_not_the_new_20), is well known for framing the journey into adulthood.

[![TedTalk - Why 30 is not the new 20](https://img.youtube.com/vi/vhhgI4tSMwc/0.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vhhgI4tSMwc "30 is not the new 20")

The twenties matter for many reasons.  During this decade:

- about 80% of a person's defining moments occur (the author's claim)
- earning power is largely set in the first 10 years of work
- about half of people marry or form a lasting partnership
- the brain changes the most
- personality changes the most
- the social network is at its biggest
- uncertainty is at its highest

# Introduction

In the 21st century, within one generation, the path has changed from leaving home, studying, graduating, marrying and raising a family in quick order, to a long gap between living in a childhood home and having a home of one's own.

| Childhood Home | *What to do with this time* | Own Home |
| :--------------: | --------------------------- | :--------: |
| *childhood* | | *adulthood* |

Essential questions are being asked during this time:

- Will I be successful?
- Will I be alone?
- Will I be loved?
- Will I be happy?
- Will my life have meaning?

The book covers three main areas: Work, Love, and the Brain and Body.  It gives 12 tools and 12 useful "learning questions", and assigns homework[^Homework] for each area.

The book ends with a story about anxiety over "Will things work out for me?".  The author starts a hike in a national park, worried about whether she will make it.  She asks the park ranger, "Am I going to make it?", and he answers, "I don't know. You haven't decided yet."

# Work

## Identity Capital

> You can't think your way through life...the only way to figure out what to do is to do something.

- [Erik Erikson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Erikson), a psychologist in the 1950s, coined the phrase [identity crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_crisis).  He was half-Jewish and half-German.  Uncertain about his vocation and his fit in society, he dropped out of school and spent a long period roaming Germany and Italy as a wandering artist with his childhood friend Peter Blos and others.  For children from prominent German families, a "wandering year" was not uncommon.
  - Erikson is best known for his theory of psychosocial development and the concept of the [identity crisis](https://www.verywellmind.com/what-is-an-identity-crisis-2795948).  His work marked a shift in thinking [on personality](https://www.verywellmind.com/facts-about-personality-2795436): instead of focusing only on early childhood, he looked at how social influences shape personality across the whole lifespan.

Identity capital is defined as:

- Our stock of personal assets.
- How we add value to who we are.
- What we have to show for how we spent our time.
- The investments we made in ourselves.

Identity capital is built by:

- **explore and commit.**  Try things, then commit to some of them.

Crisis and capital go together.  Capital builds up bit by bit over time.  Exploring and committing leads to:

- higher self-esteem
- more perseverance
- more realistic expectations
- a greater sense of self
- greater life satisfaction
- better stress management
- stronger reasoning and resistance to conformity

## Strength of Weak Ties

The thesis of *The Strength of Weak Ties*[^WeakTies] is that weak ties, meaning acquaintances who are not reinforced by many mutual friends, are especially important in the flow of information.  It has become a core idea in social network research.  In marketing, information science and politics, weak ties reach people and audiences that strong ties cannot.

The essence is that our habitual close ties do not bring new information or opportunities into our lives.  Close ties are supportive but not transformative.[^TippingPoints]

- The book says most new jobs come with help from weak ties (the commonly quoted figure is 75%; treat it as a rule of thumb, since studies differ).

| Relationship Type | Characteristics                                              |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Strong Ties       | Comfortable and familiar<br />*birds of a feather, stick together*<br />people who are like us<br />offer support and sympathy |
| Weak Ties         | Know things we do not<br />Offer something new<br />Bridges to other groups |

### Ben Franklin Effect

> *He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he whom you yourself have obliged.* — The Ben Franklin Effect 1706 - 1790

**Homophily** is the tendency of people to form connections with others who are similar to them in status, values, beliefs or attitudes.  [Nepotism](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/nepotism) is the opposite problem: favouritism, such as in hiring, based on kinship.

Why weak ties are often helpful:

- When you do something nice for someone, you like them even more afterward (social psychology).

How to approach a weak tie for help:

- Ask for a clearly defined, easily delivered favour.  Use the helper's high and the Ben Franklin effect.
- Make yourself relevant and interesting.
- Do your homework.

- the things we know about ourselves but have forgotten
- the dreams we lost sight of
- the truths we sense but don't say out loud

We fear what the unthought known means and what we should do next.  Our fear (and anxiety) is that making a choice will lead to hard work, failure and heartbreak.

The paradox of choice[^Choice-Paradox]: the example in the book is a study with two jam booths in a grocery store.  One booth had 24 choices and one had 6.

- more people stopped at the booth with 24 choices, but only about 3% of them bought jam
- fewer people stopped at the booth with 6 flavours, but about 30% of them bought jam

See [Since the book](#since-the-book): later research found this effect is much less reliable than the famous result suggests.

### The myth of talent

> When I was 15, I spent a month working on an archeological dig. I was talking to one of the archeologists one day during our lunch break and he asked those kinds of “getting to know you” questions you ask young people: Do you play sports? What’s your favorite subject? And I told him, no I don’t play any sports. I do theater, I’m in choir, I play the violin and piano, I used to take art classes.  And he went WOW. That’s amazing! And I said, “Oh no, but I’m not any good at ANY of them.”
>
> And he said something then that I will never forget and which absolutely blew my mind because no one had ever said anything like it to me before: “I don’t think being good at things is the point of doing them. I think you’ve got all these wonderful experiences with different skills, and that all teaches you things and makes you an interesting person, no matter how well you do them.”
>
>And that honestly changed my life. Because I went from a failure, someone who hadn’t been talented enough at anything to excel, to someone who did things because I enjoyed them. I had been raised in such an achievement-oriented environment, so inundated with the myth of Talent, that I thought it was only worth doing things if you could “Win” at them.” — [Kurt Vonnegut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Vonnegut)

## My Life Should Look Better on Instagram

> *Don't compare yourself to other people; compare yourself to who you were yesterday.* — Jordan Peterson, psychologist

The book links more time on social media to:

- more anxiety
- more depression
- lower self-esteem
- more disordered eating
- more fear of missing out (FOMO)
- less happiness

The key driver is *upward social comparison*, or "compare and despair".  How strong and how causal these links are is still debated (see [Since the book](#since-the-book)).

## The Search for Glory

> *The best is the enemy of the good.* — Voltaire, philosopher
>
> *Happiness is when what you think, what you say, and what you do are in harmony.* — Mahatma Gandhi

- your potential is a function of your gifts and your limitations
- scrambling for ideals leaves us alienated from ourselves and the world
- the search for glory is full of "should do..." and "supposed to...".  "Shoulds" are a <u>false dichotomy</u> between meeting an ideal and being a failure.

**Goals** lead us from within.  They are like authentic dreams.

**Expectations** judge us from the outside ("should do...", "supposed to...").  They are oppressive standards, which is not the same as high standards.

Many people in their twenties chase expectations instead of seeking their own goals.  Two reasons:

- school life is structured: you follow directions, complete assignments and tests, and get feedback as a grade
- work life is unstructured and self-directed

## The Customized Life

Distinctiveness is a fundamental part of identity:

- it is often easier to say what we are not (a negative approach)
- we need to say what we are (a positive approach)
  - an affirmative step and process
  - what are you for?  (being against something is easy)
  - claim something

The book tells the story of a person searching for their next opportunity who loves riding a custom bike.  A bike is a set of standard parts plus customizations added step by step, and a life can be built the same way.

- standard parts: education, starting a career (you can iterate on these too)
- custom parts: jobs, experiences, skills, improvements, choices (iterate!)

Claim a good story: who you are and what you want.

## Good Story

Claim a story that:

- is about your interests and talents
- you can bring to interviews and dates
- balances complexity and cohesion
  - clear communication and reasoning matter more than originality or creativity in the story
  - complexity: a story that is too simple seems inexperienced
  - cohesion: it connects your past, present and future

You have to take action and make choices.  Debating will not get you anywhere.  This fits the advice in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* to take calculated risks more often: when a choice has a positive expected contribution, the more often you make such choices, the better off you will be.  See the [Think Fast, Think Slow]({{ '/books/Think-Fast-Think-Slow/' | relative_url }}) notes.

# Love

In the 1950s the average age at first marriage was 20 for women and 22 for men.  By 2020 it had risen to 28 and 30 (figures for the United States).  In the 1950s marriage was a stepping stone to adulthood.  In 2020 it is a "capstone".

The reasons for this are:

- less religion
- more student debt
- more economic uncertainty
- more non-traditional partnerships
- ambition about work is admired

In marriage we choose a partner for money, work, lifestyle, family, health, leisure, sex, retirement and death.

In your twenties it is important to think about marriage, your criteria and what a successful relationship is.

## Picking Your Family

> *Beauty is a greater recommendation than any introduction* — attributed to Aristotle

YAVIS: Young, Attractive, Verbal, Intelligent, Successful

Marriage is still about joining families.

- these decisions affect the rest of your life
- be ambitious about love

Western culture values independence and self-fulfilment.  Be:

- ambitious about work
- ambitious about relationships
- ambitious about picking your friends
- ambitious about picking your family (relationships)

## On Dating Down

> Conversation is to be thought of as creating a social world, just as causality generates a physical one — Rom Harré, psychologist

Be serious about relationships.  Don't let your partner see a "dating down" version of you.  Know and respect your own value.

- We judge our desirability by whether we <u>think</u> other people want us, so perceived desirability becomes perceived worth.  This is flawed.

High school leaves us with some of our most defining memories.

- in our twenties, as the frontal lobe develops, we become capable of abstract thought
- in our twenties we need to start forming *<u>stories</u>* about who we are: a coherent account of the facets of our identity
  - easy: professional identity stories
  - hard: personal relationship stories.  Life stories can trap you.  Write them down and work on changing them.  Identify the people who influence you badly and the ones who influence you well.

## The Cohabitation Effect

> *Living together is not a good test for marriage*

Cohabitation in Western culture has increased over time because of:

- a more secular culture
- more student loan debt
- economic uncertainty
- unemployment among young adults
- underemployment among young adults
- availability of birth control

Effects of cohabitation:

- the book finds no evidence that living together first helps a marriage succeed
- how you choose to live together matters more than whether you do: *sliding, not deciding*
  - sliding: "It was just cheaper...", "We drifted into staying together..."
  - deciding: choosing with commitment and standards in mind (see [Since the book](#since-the-book) for later findings)

Cohabitation after engagement:

- better communication
- higher commitment
- greater marital stability
- explicit, matching levels of commitment and thoughtful joint decisions, which lead to more satisfied and faithful relationships

There is no "quick exit" from living together.  It is not low-cost or low-risk, and behavioural economics calls the result **lock-in**:

- once an investment is made, we are less likely to look at other options or change our mind
- set-up costs are the *entrance fee*, and switching costs are the time, money and effort to change
- be aware of lock-in, and routinely check your reasons for living together

## Being in Like

> *Regardless of conceptions of romantic love, the "one and only" typically lives within driving distance*

"Being in like" means:

- being alike in ways that matter
- liking who the other person is

You learn whether you like someone from a range of experiences over time.  Travelling with someone is a good test: travel anxieties, delays, uncertainty and change all show up.

We tend to pair up by *assortative mating*, matching on:

- education
- age
- ethnicity
- religion
- attractiveness
- attitudes
- values
- intelligence

Assessing a mate over time has three stages, sometimes phrased as lust, love, like:

1. Attraction
2. Kindness and goodness
3. Compatibility

Psychology says good partners are:

- emotionally stable
- agreeable
- conscientious
- open
- curious

## 29 Conversations

> *Love without conversations is impossible* — Mortimer Adler, philosopher

A partnership generally has two eras: before kids and after kids.  The 29 conversations are partnership negotiations on foundational issues:

- discuss what kind of partner you want to be, asking each other and yourself

# The Brain and the Body

## Forward Thinking

> *Life can only be understood backward, but it must be lived forward* — Søren Kierkegaard, philosopher
>
> *The more you use your brain, the more brain you will have to use* — George A. Dorsey, anthropologist

The brain:

- the front part of the brain (the frontal lobe) shapes how we act
- Phineas Gage, in 1848, survived an accident in which a steel rod went through the front of his skull and brain, but his personality changed
- the brain develops from bottom to top and from back to front, in the order of its evolution
- the **emotional brain** is the oldest part, at the base near the spine.  It holds the animal propensities and controls breathing, senses, emotions, sex, pleasure, sleep, hunger and thirst

The frontal lobe is the most forward part of the brain.

- the most recently evolved part
- the *executive functioning centre*
- logic and judgment
- cool, rational thought that balances and regulates the *hot* feelings and impulses of the emotional brain
- higher-order thinking: anticipating risk, reasoned decisions, handling probability, time and uncertainty
- the conscious work of changing habits
- forward thinking and thinking of the future
- breaking an abstract goal into concrete steps

Brain development timeline:

- about 18 months: the brain's first growth spurt, with an overproduction of connections
- around 20: the brain reaches its adult size
- 20 to 30: the frontal lobe finishes "wiring up" (a good image rather than a deadline, since development is gradual)
- about 86 billion neurons (older sources say 100 billion), each with thousands of connections

School smarts are not the same as thinking through life.  School measures how you solve structured problems and reach correct answers within a time limit.  Life smarts require forward thinking, which involves:

- how you think and act in uncertain situations
- a skill that comes with age, practice and experience

The brain can recover, has plasticity, and responds to environment and experience.

**Wiring up in your twenties.**  The twenties are a time of great risk and great opportunity, so use your brain:

- jobs
- experiences
- new skills (your twenties are the best chance to acquire them)
- we wire up what we hear and see

## A Social Experiment: Social Media

Screen time: life is better without the *compare and despair* effects of social media.  How you think about your time matters, and so does how you use it.

The book reports that lowering your screen time:

- improves sleep, so you are more rested and happier
- improves attention and focus, so you are more engaged
- frees time for things that make you happier and healthier

Be outgoing with people:

- watch movies together or stage a talent show
- stargaze
- attend lectures
- join a book club
- fashion shows
- tutor
- volunteer
- draw
- exercise

Shallow versus deep: the internet is shallow (click to the next thing); depth means giving a subject sustained attention.

Anxiety is the leading mental health issue of the twenties:

- feeling socially inadequate
- feeling socially incompetent
- feeling socially behind
- being nervous and self-conscious with people

Recommendations (time matters):

- track recreational screen time
- take time away from your phone
- think your OWN thoughts
- take 5-minute breaks

## Calm Yourself

> *When we are trying to do something new, we don't know what we are doing.  That is the biggest challenge.* — Jeffrey Kalmikoff, designer

The office is a different culture from school.  There is no freshman class or cohort you can huddle around with.

- you must work a while before being thanked or promoted
- dress codes apply
- typos matter
- sick days matter
- confidence is low when you are *underemployed*

The twenties are full of "flashbulb" memories that stay vivid for life:

- they come from emotional or unusual events
- people in their twenties naturally take these hard
- this is a "System 1" reaction to odd events.  (System 1 is the quick, intuitive thinking from *Thinking, Fast and Slow*; System 2 is the slow, rational part.)

## 20's Brain

> *The art of being wise is knowing what to overlook* — William James, psychologist

William James, often called the father of American psychology, said, "The art of being wise is knowing what to overlook."  In uncomfortable situations, learn to stop letting untrue or irrational thoughts take over.  We begin to filter out the lies and focus on the positive, true information, and over time we react less strongly to interpersonal conflict and let it be.

In the twenties the frontal lobe is still wiring up.

**Older adults** show a "positivity effect":

- more interest in positive information
- the brain reacts less strongly to negative information
- less engagement with interpersonal conflict

**Young adults**:

- look for, worry about and focus on negative information
- find bad news more upsetting and memorable than good news
- react to surprise and criticism
- risks: sustained worrying, a chronic negative state, anxiety (short term) and depression (long term)
- suppressing feelings stresses the body and brain and impairs memory

> *You can control how you interpret situations and how you respond to them.  Attitudes and reactions are the last of human freedoms.* — Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (Holocaust survivor)

Change how you think about difficult moments:

- irrational thinking imagines the worst (catastrophic thinking)
- rational thinking understands the facts and realizes the "weather" will improve

Change how you feel:

- magnify negative emotions, or calm yourself down
- resist *borrowing an ego*, such as calling your mother, which lets someone else's frontal lobe (rational thinking) do the work

## Borrowing an Ego

- letting someone else's frontal lobe do the work, for example calling mom
- if we toss our distress away like a hot potato, we never learn to handle bad days on our own.  We miss practice soothing ourselves just when the brain is best placed to learn the skill, and without it our confidence suffers
- take responsibility and "own it"

Control over emotions increases:

- life satisfaction
- optimism
- sense of purpose
- quality of relationships

## Outside In

> *Knowledge is not skill.  Knowledge + 10,000 times is skill.*

Skill, repetition and experience lead to confidence.

- mistakes are feedback
- mistakes do not define you

The book draws on Carol Dweck's growth mindset theory (see Theories).  Understanding, practising and believing in a growth mindset is essential, though later research (see [Since the book](#since-the-book)) suggests its effects are real but modest.

## Confidence

Confidence:

- comes from the outside in
- comes when you can point to things you have done on the outside
- fake confidence is "stuffing down" self-doubt
- empty confidence is platitudes
- real confidence comes from mastery experience: actual lived moments of success, especially success in spite of difficulty
  - the confidence that overrides insecurity comes from experience; master your emotions (fear and others) so you can have more mastery experiences

Confidence means "with trust": trusting yourself to get the job done, through repetition.

Confidence that survives failure comes from jobs that:

- are challenging
- require some effort
- are done without much help
- have hurdles and obstacles

At work:

- frame and hang your accomplishments (such as a diploma)
- dress professionally
- take credit for small successes
- no more self-deprecating stories

## Getting Along & Getting Ahead

> *A goal is a dream with a deadline* — Napoleon Hill, author

The twenties are the best time for change.  Personality changes a lot in the twenties; the book argues that in your thirties it changes much less.

During your twenties you tend to:

- become more conscientious
- become more responsible
- become more socially competent
- feel more agreeable about life
- feel more confident
- feel less stress
- feel less angry

Social Investment Theory (see Theories):

- becoming involved in the world around us is how we grow
- commitments to love, work and the world trigger personality maturation
- investing in adulthood makes us feel better and happier
- goals make you happier and healthier

Setting goals leads to greater purpose, mastery, agency and well-being.  They are the "*Building Blocks of Adult Personality*".

Commitments to other people foster change and well-being.

## Relationships

Relationships:

- reduce social anxiety and depression
- help us feel less lonely
- let us practise interpersonal skills
- teach us to manage emotions and resolve conflict
- are a source of security and support

## Every Body

> *I'm sorry.  It's true.  Having children really changes your view on these things.  We are born, we live for a brief instant, and we die.  Technology isn't changing it much at all.* — Steve Jobs

> *Do not confuse feminism with being allowed to want a family.* — Nancy Chodorow, 1970s

Older, educated parents are more stable, have more resources and are good for kids.  The book's caution is that fertility declines with age, so it is worth learning the facts early.

- Do not be misled by the availability heuristic.  Remembering a celebrity who had children at 40 does not reflect how common that is.  Risks of infertility and miscarriage rise with age for women, and older fathers carry a higher risk of genetic defects too.  For the medical detail, talk to a doctor.

## Do The Math

> *To achieve great things, two things are needed: a plan, and not quite enough time.* — Leonard Bernstein, composer

The brain cannot keep track of time without markers, and then we ask where the time went, confronted with mortality.  School is managed and organized into semesters, micro-goals, syllabi, assignments and tests.  Work is not.

Visualize your future self, and save and work towards it.  Avoid **present bias**: prioritizing the rewards and consequences of the here and now over the there and then.

- we would rather have $100 now than $150 in a year
- cake today, gym tomorrow
- new jeans today, credit card tomorrow
- it is hard to give weight to things that happen in the future
- people in their twenties are especially prone to it
- *memento mori* (remember you will die) versus *memento vivere* (remember you will live to your 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, 80s and beyond)

**Be future oriented.**  Build timelines and think consciously about the future, so it does not stay abstract:

- plan five years out (for example: pass the LSAT, attend law school, pass the bar exam) and take the steps, including reading about how to get into law school
- plan a 5 km run and the training for it
- plan internships
- work backwards: write your future resume, biography and obituary

# Theories

## Social Investment Theory

The [Social Investment Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_investment_theory) is a [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) theory that claims that changes in [personality traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_trait) over time are driven by changes in persons' commitments to social roles and institutions.  Since the late 1990s, there has been substantial scientific evidence that personality traits continue to change after childhood, especially during young adulthood. Generally, personality traits converge towards more [agreeableness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreeableness), [conscientiousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientiousness), and [emotional stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equanimity).

## Personality Traits: the Big Five

[Trait theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trait_theory) (also called dispositional theory) studies personality as a set of stable traits.  The [Big Five personality traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Five_personality_traits), also called the Five Factor Model, were grouped from the 1980s onward.  The acronym is OCEAN (or CANOE):

- [openness to experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Openness_to_experience) (inventive and curious vs. consistent and cautious)
- [conscientiousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientiousness) (efficient and organized vs. extravagant and careless)
- [extraversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraversion_and_introversion) (outgoing and energetic vs. solitary and reserved)
- [agreeableness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreeableness) (friendly and compassionate vs. critical and rational)
- [neuroticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroticism) (sensitive and nervous vs. resilient and confident)

Social Investment Theory predicts that, with age, people tend to become more agreeable, more conscientious and more emotionally stable.

## Theory of Emotion

[William James's theory of emotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James#Theory_of_emotion): in his oft-cited example, we do not see a bear, fear it and run; we see a bear and run, and consequently we fear the bear.  The theory holds that emotion is the mind's perception of the body's reaction to a stimulus.

### Mindset Work

[Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck) is a Stanford psychologist who researches motivation, personality and development.  In grade school, doing well on an IQ test earned privileges.  She does not think IQ tests have merit, but the motivation to do well on one had a profound impact on her life and career.  In her book [*Mindset: The New Psychology of Success* (Ballantine Books, 2008)](https://worldcat.org/en/title/967222007), she argues that people fall on a continuum according to their implicit views of where ability comes from:

- **fixed mindset:** success is based on innate ability
- **growth mindset:** success is based on hard work, learning, training and persistence; intelligence can be developed (as in *kaizen*)

> In a fixed mindset students believe their basic abilities, their intelligence, their talents, are just fixed traits. They have a certain amount and that's that, and then their goal becomes to look smart all the time and never look dumb. In a growth mindset students understand that their talents and abilities can be developed through effort, good teaching and persistence. They don't necessarily think everyone's the same or anyone can be Einstein, but they believe everyone can get smarter if they work at it.

This matters because:

1. people with a growth theory are more likely to keep working hard despite setbacks
2. people's theories of intelligence can be shaped by subtle cues in their environment

Mindset theory is related to self-actualization, part of [Maslow's hierarchy of needs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maslow%27s_hierarchy_of_needs).  [Self-actualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-actualization#Abraham_Maslow's_concept_of_self-actualization) is the highest level of psychological development, where personal potential is fully realized after basic bodily and ego needs are met.  The hierarchy from top to bottom is:

1. transcendence
2. self-actualization
3. aesthetic
4. cognitive
5. esteem
6. belonging and love
7. safety
8. physiological

Growth mindset practices:

- set personal goals
- write letters
- write about, and share with others, something you used to be poor at but are now good at
- keep a reflective journal

## What has changed
